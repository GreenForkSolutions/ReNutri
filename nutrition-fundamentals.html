Nutrition Fundamentals.

Below is a sampling of the nutritional information we impart onto our clients through visual and interactive learning activities. Many of the topics that we cover listed below are explained in a simplified easy to understand format. Our approach is to make nutrition education practical for everyone.

Macronutrients & Micronutrients

Macronutrients include protein, dietary fats, and carbohydrates. The word "macro" is a Greek term meaning "large" hence we need these three nutrients in greater amounts.

Micronutrients include vitamins and minerals and are needed in smaller amounts but are required all the same. The word "micro" is Greek for "small" helping to distinguish that even in trace amounts vitamins and minerals play an active role in health and vitality.

Protein & Amino Acids

Proteins are built from 20 unique amino acids. Nine of these are essential in the diet because our body cannot make them.

Tryptophan and phenylalanine are two essential amino acids required to produce certain neurotransmitters used in the brain, namely serotonin and dopamine. These two neurotransmitters play a fundamental role in mental health, thus emphasizing the importance of adequate protein intake.

Dietary Fats

There are four types of dietary fats and they include saturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated, and trans fats.

Polyunsaturated fats play an important role in brain health. The brain itself is made from approximately 60% fat, and special consideration for omega-3 fatty acids is warranted in particular. Plant based sources of omega-3 include hemp seeds, chia seeds, flax seeds, walnuts, cashews, and macadamia nuts. Animal sources include seafood, especially fatty fish like wild salmon and sardines. Grass-fed beef and dairy are other notable sources in comparison to conventionally raised cattle. Hens fed flaxseed meal tend to have higher levels of omega 3 in their eggs compared to other control groups but are unlikely to contain comparable amounts as those found in seafood.

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are made in plants from carbon and water, hence the term "carbo"+"hydrate." They can take form as simple carbohydrates where molecules of glucose, fructose, and galactose pair together to form common energy rich molecules like sugar (from the sugarcane plant) and lactose (from the sugar found in milk).

Complex carbohydrates are made up of much larger molecules comprised of numerous glucose molecules strung together. Starch is the most common example and contains two different versions: amylose (mostly straight chains of glucose) and amylopectin (branched chains of glucose). The many different variations of simple and complex carbohydrates can lead to differences in blood sugar, both beneficially and detrimentally. Knowing which carbohydrate rich foods are most optimally suited for good health is key, especially since carbohydrates are the brain's favourite fuel source.

Vitamins

Vitamin A

Preformed vitamin A includes retinol, retinal, and retinoic acid. Plants provide provitamin A carotenoids such as beta-carotene, alpha-carotene, and beta-cryptoxanthin. Vitamin A supports vision, immunity, and cell development.

B-complex: all eight

  • B1 (thiamin): carbohydrate energy metabolism and nerve function.
  • B2 (riboflavin): energy-producing reactions.
  • B3 (niacin): NAD/NADP coenzymes used in energy metabolism.
  • B5 (pantothenic acid): component of coenzyme A.
  • B6 (pyridoxine): amino-acid metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis.
  • B7 (biotin): enzymes involved in fat and carbohydrate metabolism.
  • B9 (folate): DNA synthesis and red blood cell formation.
  • B12 (cobalamin): nerve health, DNA synthesis, and red blood cells.

Vitamins C, D, E & K

C: antioxidant activity, collagen formation, and improved non-heme iron absorption. D: calcium absorption, bone health, and normal muscle function. E: fat-soluble antioxidant that helps protect cell membranes. K: helps activate proteins required for blood clotting from injury and supports bone metabolism.

Minerals & Electrolytes

  • Calcium: bones, teeth, muscle contraction, and nerve signalling.
  • Phosphorus: bones and teeth, ATP energy transfer, and cell membranes; phosphate also acts as an electrolyte.
  • Iron: oxygen transport in haemoglobin and energy metabolism.
  • Zinc: immune function, wound healing, and many enzymes.
  • Magnesium: enzyme activity, muscle and nerve function, and energy metabolism.
  • Manganese: enzyme function, metabolism, and antioxidant defences.
  • Potassium: fluid balance, nerve signals, and muscle function.
  • Sodium: extracellular fluid balance and nerve and muscle function.
  • Chloride: fluid and acid–base balance and stomach acid production.

Potassium, sodium, chloride, and phosphate are electrolytes; calcium and magnesium also carry electrical charges in body fluids. A varied diet usually supplies these nutrients, though individual needs differ.
